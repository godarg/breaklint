Forty-one meters and a smaller boiler

How one cold evening in December settled a six-month argument about heating the terrace.

For the maintenance committee

When the boiler in the east block failed for the third time in December, the maintenance committee did something it had never done before: it asked every household to read its own gas meter at the same hour on the same evening. Forty-one of the forty-eight flats sent in a reading, most of them within ten minutes, and the numbers that came back changed the argument that had been running since the summer.

The argument was about whether to replace the old boiler with a like-for-like model or to spend more on a heat pump for the whole terrace. The like-for-like option was cheaper and could be installed in a week. The heat pump would take a season of planning, a grant application and a vote at the general meeting, and several members worried that the co-op would end up with an expensive system that nobody understood.

The meter readings showed that the problem was not only the boiler. Flats on the north side, above the old bike store, were using almost twice as much heat as identical flats on the south side, and the difference had little to do with how warm the residents kept their rooms. It had everything to do with the uninsulated floor above the store, which had been on the repair list for eleven years.

So the committee changed the question. Instead of asking which boiler to buy, it asked what the terrace would need once the obvious leaks were fixed. The answer, worked out over three evenings with the help of a retired heating engineer from the south block, was a system about a third smaller than the one it had been planning to replace.

That made the heat pump affordable. The grant covers a little over half of the cost, the reserve fund covers most of the rest, and the remaining gap is small enough to be met by the energy savings within the first four winters. The insulation of the bike store ceiling, finally, goes ahead in April, before any new equipment is installed.

None of this would have happened without the meter readings, and the committee wants to say thank you to everyone who stood in a cold hallway with a torch on that December evening. It also wants to repeat the exercise twice a year, so that the co-op can see whether the new system does what the engineer promised. The next reading evening is on the first Tuesday of March, at eight o’clock sharp; a reminder will go under every door the week before.

Several members have asked whether the readings will be published. They will, but only as totals for each side of the terrace and each floor, never for individual flats. The spreadsheet is kept by the treasurer and can be inspected by any member on request, in the same way as the accounts.

The general meeting in May will be asked to approve the final design and the use of the reserve fund. The papers will be circulated four weeks in advance, as the rules require, and the engineer has agreed to answer questions at an open evening in the common room a fortnight before the vote.

What does the new system mean for individual flats? Very little, day to day. The radiators stay where they are, although about a third of them will be replaced with larger models because a heat pump runs at a lower water temperature than a gas boiler. The thermostats in each flat stay too. The biggest visible change is outside: the heat pump unit will stand in the service yard behind the east block, on the concrete pad where the old oil tank used to be.

The engineer has been asked about noise more than about anything else. The unit chosen is one of the quieter models on the market, and at the distance of the nearest bedroom window it should be no louder than the extractor fans that already run in the yard. A sound reading will be taken before and after installation, and the committee has agreed that the unit will be moved if the second reading is more than a few decibels above the first.

Hot water will be handled separately. Each block keeps its own cylinder, heated by the heat pump during the day and topped up by an electric immersion heater once a week to keep the water safe. The cylinders are already in place and were replaced only four years ago, so they can stay.

The timetable is tight but realistic. The bike store ceiling is insulated in April. The larger radiators go in during May and June, a few flats at a time, with at least two weeks of notice to every household. The heat pump itself is installed in the first half of July, when nobody needs heating, and the old boiler is kept as a standby until the end of the first winter.

There will be disruption. Radiator replacement means a morning without heating and with a plumber in the living room, and the pipework in the east block stairwell has to be opened up for a day. The committee will publish a schedule for each floor as soon as the installer confirms dates, and it will try to keep work in any one flat to a single visit.

Members who need the work done on a particular day, for example because of caring responsibilities or shift work, should tell the office as early as possible. The installer has agreed to fit around such requests wherever the sequence allows.

Finally, a word about cost. The co-op will not raise rents to pay for the heating works. The reserve fund exists for exactly this kind of project, and the savings on gas are expected to refill it faster than the old boiler would have allowed. The treasurer will present the full figures at the general meeting, including what happens if the savings turn out lower than forecast.

The committee is grateful to everyone who has taken part in the discussion so far, including those who disagreed. The plan is better for having been argued over, and the argument is not quite finished yet.

The garden rota returns

After a winter of frost and a flooded compost bay, the courtyard garden is ready for the new season. The rota works as before: each household takes one Saturday morning in the growing season, and swaps are arranged on the board in the laundry room. New members are paired with an experienced gardener for their first turn, which usually means an hour of weeding and an hour of conversation.

This year the garden group would like to try a small herb bed by the back gate, where the old recycling bins used to stand. The soil there needs a season of improvement first, so the bed will be planted with green manure in April and turned in before the autumn. Anyone with spare seed trays, canes or a working pair of shears is asked to leave them in the shed.

The rainwater butts have been cleaned and refitted, and the new tap on the south wall now works. Please close it after use; a dripping tap drained the whole butt in a single weekend last June.
