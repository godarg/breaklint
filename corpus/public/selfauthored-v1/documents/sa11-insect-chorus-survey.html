Acoustic survey of nocturnal insect choruses at Fenmoor Reserve

Survey report of the reserve volunteer monitoring group · one month of passive recording

Summary

Five passive acoustic recorders were left on the reserve for the thirty-one nights of July and recorded from dusk to dawn. Automated band-energy analysis, checked by listening to a random sample of one minute in fifty, shows two main choruses: field crickets calling near 4 kHz, strongest in the two hours after midnight, and bush crickets calling higher and earlier. Activity fell sharply on the three coolest nights of the month and recovered within a night once temperatures rose again.

Recorders and analysis

The recorders were strapped to fence posts at about one metre above the ground, facing away from the prevailing wind, along a line that runs from the hedge on the western boundary to the reed bed on the eastern shore. Each recorder sampled at 48 kHz and stored ten minutes of every quarter hour, which kept the memory cards and batteries sufficient for the whole month.

For each ten-minute file the analysis computed the energy in five frequency bands and expressed it relative to the quietest ten minutes of the same night at the same recorder. The resulting intensity values run from zero, for silence, to nine, for a chorus loud enough to mask everything else in the band. Birdsong at dawn and the occasional passing car were removed by hand after listening.

Results

The median intensity across all recorders and nights shows the two choruses clearly. The field cricket chorus fills the 4 kHz band from about ten in the evening and peaks between midnight and two. The bush cricket chorus appears in the 8 kHz band an hour earlier and fades sooner.

Nightly totals rise through the first half of the month, peak in the third week and decline towards the end, which matches the seasonal course of adult numbers. The dip in the fourth week coincides with three nights on which the air temperature at midnight fell below twelve degrees.

Listening to the sample files confirmed that almost all of the energy in the 4 kHz band comes from a single cricket species. Its chirp consists of six short pulses, repeated about three times a second on warm nights and more slowly as the air cools.

Discussion

A month of passive recording gave a clear picture of the two main choruses at a fraction of the effort a manual survey would need. The main weakness of the method is identification: band energy tells us that something is calling, not what. The listening sample is therefore essential, and next year it will be doubled for the 8 kHz band, where two species with similar calls may both be present.

The recorders survived the month without a single failure, but R3 lost two nights when cattle rubbed against its post and turned the microphone to face the ground. The posts will be fenced off next season.
