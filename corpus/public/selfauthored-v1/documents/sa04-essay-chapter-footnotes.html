Chapter Seven

The Weight of Small Weather

A wet morning is not an event. Thirty wet mornings in a row are a season, and a season is something you have to live through.from a harbour master’s logbook, undated

For most of the year the weather on this stretch of coast is too ordinary to talk about. It rains a little, the wind comes round from the south-west, the sea turns the colour of wet slate, and nobody who lives here would think of writing any of it down. And yet people did write it down, for a hundred and forty years, in a series of notebooks that now fill two shelves of the reading room above the lifeboat station. I spent most of one winter reading them, and this chapter is an attempt to explain why a record of nothing much turned out to be one of the most moving things I have read.

The notebooks were kept by whoever held the post of station keeper, and the post changed hands roughly once a generation. The first keeper wrote in a sloping hand that fills every line to the margin; the last, who retired when the station was automated, wrote in capitals and left half of every page blank.The station was automated in the spring of its hundred and thirty-ninth year. The final notebook ends mid-month, with a single line recording the handover of the keys. Between them are eleven other hands, some careful, some hurried, one of them so small that I read it with a magnifying glass borrowed from the stamp club that meets in the same building on Thursdays.

What they recorded was always the same: the time of each observation, the direction and force of the wind, the state of the sea, the barometer, the rainfall in the gauge, and a single word for the sky. Over the decades the single word acquired a vocabulary of its own. Dull meant an even grey cover with no rain. Heavy meant low cloud that seemed to press on the headland. Broken meant patches of blue, and clearing was used only when the change could be seen happening. There was no word for a fine day. A fine day was written as a dash.

The dash

It took me a month to understand the dash. At first I read it as an absence, a day on which the keeper had nothing to say, and I skipped over the dashes as one skips over blank lines. Then I began counting them, and found that in some years there were fewer than twenty. In the worst of the wet decades there were years with only nine. Nine days on which the keeper looked at the sky and found nothing in it worth a word.

Once I had begun to count, I could not stop. I made a table of dashes by month and by year, and then, because a table is hard to feel, I made a strip of paper for each year and marked the dashes on it in pencil.The strips are kept with the notebooks now, in a folder labelled in the librarian’s hand as a reader’s aid. They are not a scientific instrument and should not be treated as one. Laid side by side on the reading room table, the strips showed something that no single notebook could: the fine days clustered, year after year, in the last week of April and the first fortnight of September. Everything else was weather.

This is not a discovery. Anyone who has lived here for a few years could tell you that spring comes late and autumn arrives in a rush of calm days before the gales. What the strips did was to make that knowledge visible and, in a strange way, to make it belong to the people who had written the notebooks rather than to me. They had watched the same sky from the same window, and between them they had seen the pattern long before anyone thought to look for it.

* * *

There is a particular kind of attention that repetition demands. The keepers were not scientists and did not think of themselves as observers. They wrote the weather down because the rules of the post required it, in the same way that they logged the fuel for the boat engine and the number of visitors to the station on open days. But the rules could not make them look carefully, and most of them did. The notes in the margin are the proof.

The margins are where the keepers allowed themselves to write about something other than the numbers. Swallows back, in the same week of April for eleven years running. Ice on the gauge, three times in a century. Fog so thick the bell was rung all night, which happened more often than I would have guessed. One keeper noted the first cuckoo every spring and the day the last visitor left the beach every autumn, as if the two were the brackets of a single sentence.This keeper also recorded the price of coal each November, without comment. The prices, read in sequence, tell their own story about the winters.

Occasionally the margin notes break through the reserve of the form. In one notebook, beside a week of gales, the keeper has written only boat out, twice, and then, on the third day, all home. There is no other entry about the rescue. The lifeboat records, which I read afterwards, show that the crew were at sea for nineteen hours on the second night and brought back four people from a coaster that had lost its engine off the point. The weather notebook gives the wind as force nine, the sea as very high, the sky as heavy. The numbers are exactly as careful as on any other day.

Instruments

The instruments themselves survive in the station’s small museum room. The barometer is a long mercury tube in a mahogany case, with a vernier scale that the keepers read to a hundredth of an inch. The rain gauge is a copper funnel over a measuring bottle, dented where a keeper once dropped it on the rocks. The wind was estimated rather than measured: the keepers used the scale of forces that sailors use, judging the wind by its effect on the sea and on the grass of the headland.An anemometer was installed on the roof in the station’s last decade. Its readings are recorded in a separate book, and the keeper of the time went on estimating the force by eye in the old notebook as well.

It would be easy to be sentimental about these objects, and I will try not to be. They were ordinary instruments of their period, bought from a catalogue and maintained according to a printed card that is still pinned inside the barometer case. The card tells the keeper to tap the glass gently before reading, to keep the gauge clear of leaves, and to report any damage to the district office. It does not tell the keeper to look at the sky and choose a word for it. That part was left to them.

I have come to think that the single word was the most important measurement in the notebooks, precisely because no instrument produced it. The barometer and the gauge give numbers that could have been taken by anyone. The word for the sky had to be chosen, every morning, by someone standing at the window, and over a hundred and forty years the choices add up to something like a shared way of seeing.

Wind west, fresh. Sea moderate. Glass steady. Sky broken, clearing by noon. Gauge nil. Two boats out at first light for the lobster pots, both home by ten.

That entry is typical. It is also, if you read enough of them, oddly beautiful, in the way that a well-made tool is beautiful. There is nothing in it that does not need to be there. The boats are mentioned because the keeper was responsible for knowing who was at sea. The clearing is mentioned because it was seen. The gauge is empty and says so.

The last notebook

The final notebook is the thinnest. The last keeper had held the post for twenty-six years and knew that the station would be automated before the book was full. The entries grow shorter as the date approaches, and in the final month the single word for the sky is often the only thing written after the numbers. On the last page there is a week of dashes, the longest run of fine weather in the whole series, and then the handover.The run of fine days falls in the second week of September, exactly where the strips predict it.

I do not know what the keeper felt, writing those dashes. The notebook does not say, and it would be a kind of trespass to guess. What I can say is that the record is complete. Every morning of those hundred and forty years has its line, including the mornings when the keeper was ill and a neighbour took the readings, and the morning when the window was broken by a gale and the keeper stood outside to look at the sky.

The automatic station records the weather every ten minutes now, and its readings are more accurate than anything in the notebooks. It measures the wind with a cup anemometer, the rain with a tipping bucket and the sky with a sensor that counts sunlight. It has no word for heavy, and no word for broken, and it does not leave a dash for a fine day. It does not need to. Nobody is standing at the window any more.The station’s readings are published online and can be downloaded in a spreadsheet, which is how I checked the keeper’s estimates of wind force against the machine for the few years in which both were kept.

When I finished reading, I returned the notebooks to their shelves in order and put the strips of paper back in their folder. On the way home, walking along the sea wall, I caught myself looking at the sky and trying to choose a word for it. The word I chose was dull, and it was exactly right.

This chapter is a work of fiction written for a specimen document. The coast, the station, its keepers and the book it belongs to are invented.
