1 Why we survey the banks

A riverbank changes slowly enough that nobody notices, and quickly enough that a decade of neglect can move a footpath into the water. The volunteer survey exists to make those changes visible. Twice a year, small teams walk the same sixteen transects along Lantern Creek, read the same pins, photograph the same stretches of bank from the same spots and fill in the same form. None of this is exciting on the day. Its value lies entirely in the repetition, which is why this handbook asks you to do things in a fixed way even where another way would seem just as good.

The records serve three purposes. They tell the Trust where the bank is retreating fast enough to threaten the path, the old mill race or the alder stand below the footbridge. They show whether the willow spiling installed after the flood four winters ago is holding the toe of the slope. And they give the county drainage office, which has no survey staff of its own on this reach, a long series of observations that nobody else collects.

You do not need training in geomorphology or botany to take part. You need a steady hand with a ruler, a willingness to kneel in wet grass, and the patience to write numbers down before you interpret them. The chapters that follow explain what to bring, what the bank profile means, how to read erosion pins and vegetation quadrats, and how to send the sheet back so that it reaches the database intact.

Safety first. Never survey alone, never enter the channel when the water is above the second marker on the footbridge gauge, and turn back if the bank top is undercut where you intend to stand. A missed reading can be repeated next season; an accident cannot be undone.

2 Before you go

Check the survey calendar on the noticeboard at the boathouse and sign up for a transect pair. Each pair is surveyed by two people who stay together for the whole visit. Collect the survey bag from the key box the evening before; it contains the ruler, the quadrat frame, spare pencils and a laminated copy of this handbook. Please return everything to the bag before you hand it back, including the pencil stubs.

Dress for the ground rather than for the weather forecast. The lower bank stays wet long after rain has stopped, and the nettle beds near transects nine to eleven reach waist height by June. Gloves are strongly recommended for the litter count.

What to bring

  • the survey bag, checked against the contents card inside its lid;
  • a phone or camera with enough free storage for sixteen photographs;
  • a clipboard with two blank field sheets and a waterproof cover;
  • drinking water, a first aid kit and the emergency contact card;
  • a watch, because every reading is logged with the time it was taken.

If the creek is running high on the morning of your visit, postpone rather than improvise. Readings taken on a flooded bank are not comparable with the rest of the series, and the database flags them as suspect automatically.

3 Reading the bank profile

Every transect starts at a numbered steel pin driven into the bank top and runs at right angles to the flow down to the water line. The profile you see when you stand beside the pin is rarely a simple slope. Most of the Lantern Creek banks have an upper slope covered in grass, a steeper scour face where the water has cut into the soil during floods, and a gentler toe where material that fell from above has come to rest.

The boundary between the upper slope and the scour face is the line to watch. When it moves towards the pin, the bank is retreating. When grass and sedge begin to colonise the scour face, the bank is recovering. Neither process is uniform along the creek, which is why sixteen transects are surveyed rather than two or three.

When you arrive at a transect, stand still for a moment and look before you measure. Note anything that has changed since the photograph in the survey bag was taken: a fallen branch lodged against the toe, fresh cattle tracks, a new slump. These observations go into the notes field and are often more useful to the Trust than the numbers themselves.

4 Recording erosion and vegetation

Recording bank-top vegetation

Taking the photo points

Record the exposed length of each pin before you touch the vegetation, because kneeling on the bank top can compress the soil around the pin by a millimetre or two. Then lay the quadrat frame against the pin, upstream edge first, and work outwards square by square. For each square, write down the plant group that covers the largest part of it. If two groups look equal, choose the one closer to the water; the rule is arbitrary, but applying it consistently keeps the series comparable.

Photographs complete the record. Take them last, when the bank has been disturbed as little as possible by your work, and check each one on the screen before moving on. A blurred photograph of a bank that has since changed cannot be retaken.

5 Sending your records

At the end of the visit, check the sheet together with your partner. Every field must hold either a value or a dash; an empty field is read as a missed measurement and the whole transect is flagged. Sign the sheet, photograph it and send the photograph to the records inbox listed on the contact card. Post the paper original in the letterbox at the boathouse within seven days.

The records coordinator enters the values twice, independently, and compares the two entries before anything reaches the database. If a value looks implausible, for instance a pin that has gained cover on a bank that was visibly eroding, the coordinator will contact you. This is not a criticism. More often than not the explanation is a fallen clod that buried the pin, and your notes are what allow the coordinator to decide.

6 Glossary

Bank top
The point where the level ground of the floodplain begins to fall towards the channel.
Erosion pin
A steel rod of known length driven horizontally or vertically into the bank; the exposed length shows how much soil has been lost around it.
Quadrat
One square of the survey frame, twenty centimetres on each side.
Riffle
A shallow stretch where the water runs fast over gravel.
Scour face
The steep, often bare part of the bank that floodwater has cut into.
Spiling
Live willow rods woven between stakes at the toe of the bank; they root and hold the soil.
Toe
The foot of the bank, where fallen material collects at the water line.
Transect
A fixed line from the bank-top pin to the water, surveyed in the same way on every visit.
