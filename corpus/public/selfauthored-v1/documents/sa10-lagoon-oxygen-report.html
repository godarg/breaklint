Sediment oxygen demand in three shallow lagoons: an interim technical report

Field and laboratory team, Carrow Basin Hydrology Laboratory

Carrow Basin Hydrology Laboratory, Technical Report 24-07. Interim results from the first sampling season; not peer reviewed.

Summary. Sediment oxygen demand was measured monthly from March to September at six stations in the three Wexmoor lagoons using intact core incubations. Demand rose from spring to a peak in July at every station and was highest in the North lagoon, which receives the drainage from the old sewage works. Across stations, oxygen uptake tracked the organic carbon content of the upper sediment closely. A storm in September resuspended the sediment at the outfall station and produced the single highest reading of the season. The results support the planned dredging of the North lagoon but suggest that the South lagoon can be left undisturbed.

1 Introduction

The Wexmoor lagoons are three connected basins, each less than two metres deep, that were cut for salt production and have been left to fill with sediment for more than a century. In warm, still weather the water above the sediment can lose most of its oxygen within a day, and the fish kills of two recent summers prompted the basin authority to ask how much of that oxygen loss is caused by the sediment itself rather than by the water column.

Sediment oxygen demand is the rate at which oxygen is taken up by the bottom sediment through respiration of organisms living in it and through chemical oxidation of reduced compounds released from deeper layers. In shallow, poorly flushed water it can dominate the oxygen budget. This report presents the first season of monthly measurements and compares the three lagoons.

2 Study sites

Two coring stations were placed in each lagoon, one near the inflow and one near the outflow. Station S6 lies at the head of the outfall channel, where the lagoons drain to the estuary through a tidal sluice. The North lagoon receives the drainage of the former sewage works, which closed nine years ago but whose outfall channel still carries seepage after heavy rain.

3 Methods

At each visit three intact cores were taken at each station with a clear acrylic tube of 9 cm internal diameter, keeping about 15 cm of sediment and 20 cm of overlying water. The cores were sealed, returned to the laboratory within two hours and incubated in the dark at the water temperature measured in the field. Dissolved oxygen in the overlying water was recorded every ten minutes with an optical sensor while a small magnetic stirrer kept the water mixed without disturbing the sediment surface.

Oxygen uptake was calculated from the linear part of the decline in concentration, normally the first three to five hours, and scaled to the sediment area of the core:

SOD = (V / A) · (ΔC / Δt)

where V is the volume of overlying water, A the area of the core and ΔC / Δt the rate of change of oxygen concentration. Organic carbon was measured on the upper two centimetres of a fourth core by loss on ignition, calibrated against a set of samples analysed by combustion.

4 Results

Sediment oxygen demand followed the same seasonal course at all stations, rising steadily through the spring and peaking in July, when the water temperature was highest. The North lagoon had the highest demand throughout the season and the South lagoon the lowest, with the Middle lagoon between them.

The single highest reading of the season came from station S6 in September, two days after a storm had stirred up the sediment at the head of the outfall channel. The freshly exposed sediment took up oxygen at more than twice the rate measured there in August. Across all stations and months, oxygen uptake rose almost linearly with the organic carbon content of the upper sediment (Figure 4). The relationship held within each lagoon as well as between them, which suggests that the supply of organic matter, rather than any difference in the sediment fauna, controls the demand.

5 Discussion

The measurements confirm that the sediment of the North lagoon is a large sink for oxygen, large enough on its own to explain the low oxygen concentrations recorded there on calm summer nights. The close link between demand and organic carbon points to the old sewage outfall as the ultimate source: the carbon-rich layer deposited while the works were operating is still being respired.

Removing that layer by dredging, as the basin authority proposes, should reduce the demand of the North lagoon towards the level of the Middle lagoon. The South lagoon shows no such legacy, and its demand is low enough that dredging it would cause more disturbance than it would prevent. The storm reading at S6 is a reminder that dredging itself will resuspend sediment and raise the demand for a time; the work should be scheduled for the cool months, when the effect on oxygen will be smallest.

The second sampling season will add winter measurements and repeat the storm comparison if a suitable event occurs. Porewater profiles will be taken at two stations to separate the biological and chemical parts of the demand.
